What size solar system do I actually need? The 5-minute sizing method.

Bruno de Madrazo
Updated July 26, 2026·How we calculate this

The installer salesperson will quote you a system based on roof space, not your actual energy needs. Here is the honest sizing math, derived from 12 months of your utility bills and the local PVWatts production figure. It takes five minutes and saves you from the two most expensive mistakes: oversizing for export-poor net metering, and undersizing right before you buy an EV.

TL;DR: For a typical US home using 10,800 kWh/yr in a 1,450 kWh/kW state (the 50-state average): 7.4 kW. Bump to 9.3 kW (125% target) if you'll add an EV. Bump to 10.8 kW + battery (145%) if you'll add a heat pump too. Don't oversize beyond 25% above current usage — it hurts the math in 2026. Sunnier state, smaller array: the same home needs only 6.1 kW in California (1,775 kWh/kW) and 9.4 kW in Washington (1,153).

The one-line sizing formula

system_kW = (annual_kWh × coverage_target) ÷ state_kWh_per_kW

Three inputs, one number out. Let's break each one down.

Input 1 — annual kWh

Pull 12 months of bills from your utility account. Most US utilities offer Green Button CSV download. Add the kWh column. Don't trust a single month — summer AC or winter heat skews wildly.

Sanity benchmarks (US 2026 averages from EIA):

  • Apartment / condo, no AC: 3,500-5,500 kWh/yr
  • Townhouse, gas heat, moderate AC: 7,000-9,500 kWh/yr
  • Single-family, gas heat, central AC: 10,000-13,000 kWh/yr
  • Single-family, all-electric (heat pump): 15,000-22,000 kWh/yr
  • Single-family with EV (12,500 mi/yr): add ~3,500 kWh/yr
  • Single-family with pool pump: add ~2,000 kWh/yr

Input 2 — coverage target

How much of your usage you want solar to cover annually. The breakdown for 2026:

ScenarioRecommended target
Net-metered state (most US), no EV/heat pump plans100% of current usage
Net-metered state, planning EV in <5 yr115-125% of current usage
Net-metered, planning EV + heat pump130-150% of current usage
California NEM 3.0 (post-April 2023)95-105% + battery
Hawaii, parts of TX (no net metering)100% self-consumption + battery

Input 3 — kWh per kW per year (state factor)

This is the NREL PVWatts state average — peak sun hours × 365 × a 0.80 system derate. All 50 states are in our Solar Payback Index dataset (column kwh_per_kw_yr); these are the bands the dataset falls into:

  • Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada (desert Southwest): 1,870-1,910 kWh/kW
  • California, Hawaii: 1,700-1,775 kWh/kW
  • Colorado, Oklahoma, Florida: 1,600-1,655 kWh/kW
  • Texas, North Carolina, Alabama: 1,520-1,535 kWh/kW
  • Massachusetts, New Jersey, Illinois: 1,315-1,375 kWh/kW
  • Maine, Michigan, Vermont: 1,270-1,330 kWh/kW
  • Washington, Oregon (cloudy PNW): 1,150-1,190 kWh/kW

The 50-state average is 1,453 kWh/kW/yr; the spread runs from Alaska's 926 to Arizona's 1,910 — more than a 2× difference, which is why a national rule of thumb sizes the wrong array almost everywhere. For your exact ZIP code, use the NREL PVWatts calculator — it accounts for roof azimuth, tilt and weather. Default tilt = latitude.

Worked examples

Example 1 — typical Sacramento household, no upgrades planned. 10,200 kWh/yr × 100% coverage ÷ 1,775 (California) = 5.7 kW. Round up to 6 kW. 14 panels at 440 W.

Example 2 — Atlanta household, will buy an EV next year. 9,800 kWh/yr current + 3,500 future EV = 13,300 kWh effective × 115% buffer ÷ 1,510 (Georgia) = 10.1 kW. Round to 10 kW. 23 panels at 440 W.

Example 3 — Boston household considering full electrification. 9,500 kWh/yr current + 3,500 EV + 7,000 heat pump (offsetting gas) = 20,000 kWh × 105% ÷ 1,372 (Massachusetts) = 15.3 kW. That's at the edge of roof space — most likely 13-14 kW + a 10 kWh battery, accepting some grid backup.

Example 4 — San Diego SDG&E household, NEM 3.0. 8,800 kWh × 100% ÷ 1,775 (California) = 5.0 kW. With NEM 3.0 the export is worthless, so resist the installer's pitch for 8 kW "future-proofing". Right-size at 5 kW plus a 13.5 kWh Powerwall 3.

Every divisor above is the state's kwh_per_kw_yr from our Solar Payback Index dataset — the same figure the Solar ROI calculator preloads when you pick that state, so the sizing you get here and the payback you get there are built on one number.

Sizing the typical US household, step by step

Inputs: a single-family home using 10,800 kWh/yr (12-month bill total), a 100% coverage target (net-metered state, no EV or heat pump planned), and a state production factor of 1,450 kWh per kW per year — the 50-state average in our dataset (1,453, rounded). Your own state will differ: California is 1,775, Washington 1,153. Same numbers as the TL;DR — here is every step.

System size — (10,800 kWh × 100%) ÷ 1,450 kWh/kW7.4 kW
Expected production — 7.4 kW × 1,450 kWh/kW10,730 kWh/yr
Coverage achieved — 10,730 ÷ 10,80099.4%
Panel count — ceil(7,400 W ÷ 430 W panels)18 panels
Result: a 7.4 kW array (18 × 430 W panels) produces about 10,730 kWh/yr — covering 99.4% of this home's usage, just as designed. Add a buffer only if an EV or heat pump is coming; for a flat load, this is the right size and oversizing it just wastes panels.

From kW to panel count

Residential panels in 2026 are 400 W to 450 W per panel. Tier-1 standard (Q-Cells, REC, Silfab): 415-435 W. Premium (Maxeon, REC Alpha Pure-R): 440-460 W.

panel_count = ceil(system_kW × 1000 ÷ panel_watts)

A 9 kW system at 440 W panels = 9000 ÷ 440 = 20.45, round up to 21 panels. Each panel needs ~18-20 sq ft of unshaded roof at correct azimuth.

Inverter sizing (DC/AC ratio)

Most installers ship a system with a DC/AC ratio of 1.15-1.25, meaning the inverter is undersized vs panel nameplate. This is fine — peak DC output rarely happens (clouds, temperature, angle) so the inverter clips a few hours per year. Don't pay extra for a larger inverter unless you live somewhere clear-sky like Arizona; the math doesn't pencil.

Should you size up or size down?

Size up if…

  • You're in a net-metered state and an EV is coming in <5 years — add ~3,500 kWh/yr of load now (target 115–125%).
  • You plan to swap gas heat for a heat pump — that's another ~7,000 kWh/yr to offset (target 130–150%).
  • Your roof has clear, well-oriented space to spare, so the marginal panels are cheap per watt.
  • Your utility export credit is genuine net metering, so any surplus is worth close to retail.

Size down if…

  • You're on California NEM 3.0 or a no-net-metering state — surplus exports for ~$0.05 while imports cost ~$0.32.
  • Your load is flat (no EV or electrification planned) — past 100% coverage you're paying for kWh you give away.
  • Your utility caps residential systems at 105–115% of historical usage and will deny interconnection above it.
  • Usable roof is limited or shaded — don't add north-slope panels just to hit a number; right-size and add a battery instead.

When NOT to oversize

  1. NEM 3.0 California, no battery. Surplus exported gets ~$0.05/kWh while imported is $0.32. Don't make extra you can't use.
  2. Texas / Hawaii / state without net metering. Same logic — surplus is worthless without an export credit.
  3. Restrictive utility size caps. Some utilities cap residential at 105-115% of historical usage. Oversize and they'll deny interconnection.
  4. Roof can't hold it. Check shading + azimuth — don't add panels on the north-facing slope just to hit a number.

Frequently asked questions

How many kW of solar do I need for my home?

Divide annual kWh usage (12-month bills) by your state's kWh/kW. For 10,800 kWh/yr at the 1,450 kWh/kW 50-state average: 7.4 kW. In California (1,775) the same home needs 6.1 kW; in Washington (1,153), 9.4 kW.

Should I oversize my solar array?

In net-metered states: oversize 15-25% if planning future EV/heat pump load. In NEM 3.0 California or non-net-metered states: right-size + battery — oversizing makes no sense without proper export credit.

How many panels is that?

Modern residential panels: 400-450 W. Divide system kW by panel watts. A 7.4 kW system at 430 W = 18 panels. Typical US roof fits 30-40 max.
